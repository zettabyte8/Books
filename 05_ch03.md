# 3. The Cognitive Toolkit

**Objectives.** Operate seven compliance principles; explain five judgement biases; distinguish a heuristic from a fallacy; explain the illusory truth effect; describe how the Overton window is shifted.

**Key terms:** reciprocity · commitment · social proof · authority · liking · scarcity · unity · availability · anchoring · loss aversion · illusory truth · Overton window

---

Dark methods are not magic. They pull levers that exist in every brain because they were useful once. Knowing the levers is the whole of Chapter 11's defence.

Robert Cialdini's *Influence* (1984) catalogued six, and added a seventh, **unity**, in 2016:

1. **Reciprocity** — an unrequested gift creates debt. Free samples, free trials, favours logged for later.
2. **Commitment and consistency** — a small public yes constrains the next answer. Petition signatures before door-knocking; the "foot in the door."
3. **Social proof** — under uncertainty, copy the majority. Laugh tracks, bestseller lists, review counts, bot swarms.
4. **Authority** — credentials substitute for evaluation. White coats, titles, "doctors recommend."
5. **Liking** — familiarity and similarity increase compliance. Influencer parasociality.
6. **Scarcity** — shortage signals value. Countdown timers, "3 left."
7. **Unity** — shared identity, not mere similarity. "One of us." The strongest and least visible.

Alongside these sit the biases behavioural economics mapped:

- **Availability heuristic** — probability judged by how easily an example comes to mind. This is why vivid rare risks beat dull common ones.
- **Anchoring** — the first number contaminates every later estimate.
- **Loss aversion** — losses hurt roughly twice as much as equivalent gains please. Hence "don't lose your discount."
- **Framing** — 90% survival and 10% mortality are the same fact with different politics.
- **Illusory truth** — repetition alone raises belief, *even when the source is known to be unreliable and the claim is known to be false*. This is the actual mechanism under the "Big Lie": the claim is not argued into you, it is worn into you.

### The window

Not every campaign aims at a belief. Some aim at the *range of options considered speakable*, and that is a different target with different tactics.

Joseph Overton, working at a Michigan think tank in the mid-1990s, described policy ideas as arranged along a spectrum from unthinkable to popular, with only a narrow band currently acceptable. Influence then has three moves that never require winning the argument:

- **Normalise** — fund and platform the fringe position until it stops sounding fringe. Presence reads as plausibility.
- **Triangulate** — define the centre so that your preferred option sits inside it, and the incumbent option falls outside.
- **Exhaust** — make the opposing option tedious rather than wrong. Boredom moves windows faster than refutation.

The strategic payoff is that a shifted window delivers a whole programme later, while a won argument delivers one decision now.

⚠️ **Overton caution.** The window is a think-tank heuristic, not a measured quantity — nobody can size one, and no peer-reviewed instrument exists. It is also self-flattering: every advocate claims to be widening one, and the metaphor is used to claim more than it can carry. Use it to describe *sequencing*, not to prove success.

⚠️ **Contested: is all of this "manipulation"?** Dual-process theory has been challenged. A large 2020 replication found the classic System 1/System 2 *ordering* effects weaker than claimed, and some researchers argue the two systems are better described as one process under different loads. The biases listed above survive; the tidy two-mind story does not. Teach the effects, hedge the metaphor.
