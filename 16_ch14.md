# 14. Law, Institutions, Ethics

**Objectives.** Name the four regulatory levers; apply the TARES test; state the publicity principle; identify three unethical nudge designs.

**Key terms:** DSA · ad library · data minimisation · C2PA · TARES · publicity principle · fiduciary design

---

Individual literacy cannot outrun a billion-dollar system alone. Four levers exist, and each has a cost.

**1. Transparency and audit.** The EU Digital Services Act (Reg. 2022/2065) obliges very large platforms to assess systemic risk, offer a non-profiled feed, and open data to vetted researchers. Public ad libraries disclose who paid for political ads and how they were targeted.

**2. Privacy limits.** GDPR and its analogues restrict profiling on sensitive categories and require a lawful basis.

**3. Provenance.** The C2PA standard embeds cryptographic signatures in capture and edit, so a file can attest its own history. Adopted by major camera makers; useless unless the platform displays it.

**4. Pluralism.** Antitrust action, public-service charters, and support for local newsrooms keep a shared factual floor.

**Costs.** Transparency regimes generate compliance theatre; privacy law entrenches incumbents with legal teams; provenance breaks on screenshot; pluralism subsidies can capture. None is a clean win, and all four are slow. Note also that levers 1 to 3 are European in origin and reach only where Brussels has jurisdiction — a gap Chapters 9 to 11 make plain, and one that leaves the South Asian forwarding economy and the Ethiopian licence desk untouched.

**Ethics.** Sherry Baker and David Martinson's **TARES** test (2001) is the standard instrument:

- **T**ruthfulness of the message
- **A**uthenticity of the persuader
- **R**espect for the persuadee
- **E**quity of the appeal
- **S**ocial responsibility for the common good

The **publicity principle** is the operational version: would the designer defend the exact mechanism, in public, to the people being steered? If disclosure kills the effect, the effect was deception.

**Three designs that fail.** The pre-ticked consent box (inauthentic). The roach-motel cancellation (disrespectful). The countdown timer that resets on reload (untruthful).

⚠️ **Contested: is nudging ethical at all?** Thaler and Sunstein say yes — freedom of choice survives. Objectors (notably Bovens, and Gigerenzer on "boosting" as an alternative) argue nudges exploit exactly the irrationality they claim to respect, and that teaching competence beats steering it. Both are defensible. The TARES test will separate most real cases, which is why it is here rather than a verdict.

📄 **Primary source.** Baker & Martinson (2001), *Journal of Mass Media Ethics*. Note that they wrote it about advertising, before algorithms existed.

**Check.** (1) Which lever fails if the platform doesn't render provenance? (2) Apply TARES to a pre-ticked box, criterion by criterion. (3) What is the difference between nudging and boosting?

🔧 **Activity.** Draft a one-page disclosure standard for your school's own communications. It must pass the publicity principle and be short enough to read aloud in assembly.

**Summary.** Four levers, four costs, one test. Ethics is not a mood; it is a checklist you can be held to.
