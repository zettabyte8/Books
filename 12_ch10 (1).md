# 10. Scale: South Asia's Platform Elections

**Objectives.** Explain platform-party machinery as a fourth arrangement; describe the WhatsApp distribution model; assess three regulatory responses; distinguish scale from persuasion.

**Key terms:** platform-party machinery · forwarding economy · zero-rating · PEMRA · periodicals registration

---

The fourth arrangement owns no transmitter. It rents a network, staffs it with volunteers, and distributes through forwarding.

**India** is the largest case. The 2019 general election ran through a party volunteer network reported at around 900,000 WhatsApp groups, with material pushed downward and screenshots pushed back up as proof of reach. WhatsApp is close to synonymous with the internet for a large share of Indian users, and the Reuters Institute's Digital News Report has tracked it for years as the primary news source for a substantial minority. The state's regulator is largely irrelevant to this traffic: no licence, no must-carry rule, no disclosure of who paid for a forward.

The distribution model has a signature failure mode. In 2018, rumour-driven lynchings led WhatsApp to cap forwarding at five chats and label forwarded messages — a platform patching a national information system because no regulator could. In 2019 the cap was lifted during the election, then restored.

**Pakistan** runs the older model alongside the new. PEMRA, the electronic media regulator under the 2002 ordinance, has issued suspension orders against channels and, in 2022, briefly blocked a broadcaster's transmission; press-freedom organisations document a pattern of closure threats over coverage of the military and judiciary. Here carriage is still the lever, and it is administrative rather than commercial.

**Bangladesh** shows the statutory route hardening. The Digital Security Act 2018 was used heavily against journalists and citizens for online speech, with detentions under its vaguely drafted provisions; it was replaced in 2023 by a Cyber Security Act that retained much of the machinery while trimming some penalties.

**India's own statutory layer** moved in 2023 with the Press and Registration of Periodicals Act, replacing a colonial-era 1867 framework. It simplifies titles but keeps a registration gate — a nineteenth-century instrument updated, not retired.

**What this chapter is really about.** Scale is not the same as persuasion. Nine hundred thousand groups is a distribution fact; whether it changes votes is a separate question, and the honest answer is that the effect is real, unevenly measured, and heavily confounded by the same demographic targeting problems Chapter 12 discusses. Two neighbours in the same city can be in different groups receiving different claims, and neither can see the other's — the public sphere fractures before any argument is made.

⚠️ **Contested: how much does the machinery persuade?** The volunteer-network accounts are largely journalistic and self-reported by parties, which inflates them. Academic work on Indian WhatsApp finds high exposure but limited verification behaviour, and the causal step from exposure to vote is rarely isolated. Treat the group counts as order-of-magnitude, and treat "it swung the election" as unproven for any single election you can name.

⚠️ **Contested: is the forwarding economy uniquely South Asian?** Partly. It travels with cheap data, low home broadband, and languages badly served by legacy media — conditions also found in Brazil, Indonesia, and Nigeria. The mechanism is general; the intensity is not. That distinction is what stops the chapter becoming exoticism.
