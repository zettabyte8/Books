# 1. What Mass Influence Is, and Who Studies It

**Objectives.** By the end of this chapter you can: define mass influence and distinguish it from one-to-one persuasion; name six disciplines that study it and what each contributes; state three classic experiments *and* their main criticisms.

**Key terms:** mass influence · crowd psychology · conformity · obedience · cognitive dissonance · manufactured consent

---

Mass influence is the deliberate shaping of what a large population believes, feels, or does, by actors who cannot talk to each person individually. It is not a modern invention and not a single field. It is a problem that six disciplines have each attacked from a different angle — and the split matters, because each one sees something the others miss.

**Crowd psychology** studies how people change inside groups. Gustave Le Bon's *The Crowd* (1895) argued that crowds dissolve individual judgement into suggestion and contagion. Modern work rejects his contempt for "the mob" but confirms the underlying effect: high-arousal group settings synchronise emotion and lower inhibition.

**Social psychology** supplies the experiments. Solomon Asch (1951) showed students denying the evidence of their own eyes to match a unanimous group. Stanley Milgram (1961–63) showed ordinary people administering what they believed were dangerous shocks on instruction from a man in a lab coat. Leon Festinger's cognitive dissonance theory (1957) explains the aftermath: people adjust belief to justify action already taken, which is why small initial commitments are so powerful.

**Public relations and propaganda studies** study framing at scale. Walter Lippmann (*Public Opinion*, 1922) argued citizens act on "pictures in their heads" built by others, and that whoever builds those pictures governs the reaction. Edward Bernays turned this into a trade.

**Behavioural economics** maps the shortcuts. Kahneman and Tversky's System 1 / System 2 distinction — fast intuition versus slow analysis — explains why campaigns address feeling rather than argument.

**Political science** studies structure: who owns the channel, who is quoted, who absorbs the backlash. Herman and Chomsky's propaganda model (1988) and Robert Proctor's agnotology — the deliberate manufacture of doubt — belong here.

**Cybernetics and computational social science** treat a population as a signal to be measured and corrected in real time. Norbert Wiener's *Cybernetics* (1948) is the ancestor of every engagement dashboard.

⚠️ **Contested: the classic experiments are shakier than the textbooks say.** Milgram's obedience rate has been revised downward by archival work (Perry, *Behind the Shock Machine*, 2012) and by a 2019 replication finding obedience well below the original 65%. Asch's line studies have been criticised for small samples and a single-culture base. Zimbardo's Stanford Prison Experiment is the weakest of the three: the guards were coached, and the study has no control group. Use all four, but teach them as *demonstrations*, not measurements.

📄 **Primary source.** Asch, S. E. (1951), "Effects of group pressure upon the modification and distortion of judgments." Read the method section, then ask: how many trials, and how were confederates seated?

**Check.** (1) Why does it matter which discipline owns a question? (2) Name two reasons the Milgram result is now uncertain. (3) What is the difference between conformity for acceptance and conformity from assumed superior knowledge?

🔧 **Activity.** Run a five-person Asch replication with card stock and a stopwatch. Report your rate. Compare it to 1951 and to 2019, and explain the gap in one paragraph.

**Summary.** Six disciplines, one object. Each contributes a real lens and each has a blind spot. The experiments are demonstrations of possibility, not fixed constants — which is exactly why they are worth studying.
