# 9. Elsewhere: State Media Beyond the Atlantic

**Objectives.** Distinguish four structural arrangements of state media; explain why carriage control substitutes for disclosure law; assess the Al Jazeera case; map a national information system onto the four architectures.

**Key terms:** state monopoly broadcaster · cadena · carriage control · clientelism · platform-party machinery

---

Everything in Chapters 5 to 8 happened in Europe or the United States, where disclosure law is at least nominally present. Most of the world runs without it. The four architectures of Chapter 2 do not disappear — they invert. Interface control is heavy, institutional disclosure is thin, and the decisive lever is not transparency but **carriage**: who gets the slot, the frequency, the cable package, the group chat.

Four arrangements recur. They are distinguishable, and the distinction is the analytic work of this chapter.

**1. State monopoly broadcaster.** Venezuela's *cadenas* are the sharpest example: mandatory simultaneous broadcast of the president, pre-empting every other channel, which monitors counted in the dozens of hours per week at their peak. The mechanism needs no argument and no disclosure — it needs a transmitter and a decree. Soviet agitprop (→ 6) is the ancestor.

**2. State-funded, editorially semi-independent.** Al Jazeera, launched in 1996 on the Emir of Qatar's money, broke the regional taboo on criticising Arab governments and carried the 2011 uprisings when state broadcasters would not. It also demonstrated the arrangement's ceiling: it was instrumentalised during the 2017 blockade, its Arabic and English services diverge, and it remains structurally reluctant to bite the hand.

**3. Private monopoly in a clientelist deal.** Here the state owns nothing and owes something. Brazil's Globo built a near-national audience under military rule and, by most accounts, lent it. Mexico's Televisa reached a documented 2012 arrangement with the Peña Nieto campaign covering favourable coverage, reported in the *New York Times* in 2014. The lever is the licence, the debt, and the advertising book — not the statute.

**4. Platform-party machinery.** India's 2019 election ran through a volunteer network reported at around 900,000 WhatsApp groups; Brazil's 2018 campaign ran through WhatsApp in a country where the app is the internet. Here the party rents the network, the regulator is largely irrelevant, and forwarding is the distribution model. WhatsApp's own 2018 forwarding limits came after rumour-driven lynchings — a platform patching a state's information system.

**The hinge case.** In May 2017, planted quotes attributed to the Emir appeared on Qatar's state news agency site. Within days Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt had severed relations and shut Al Jazeera bureaux. A media hack became a casus belli. Nothing in Chapters 7 or 8 has an equivalent: a forged wire story producing a blockade in seventy-two hours.

**The lesson for the student.** A reader trained only on Digital Services Act transparency (→ 12) will systematically over-read a WhatsApp election and under-read a licence renewal. Chapter 2's warning applies at full force: analyse one register and you under-report the pressure.

⚠️ **Contested: does Al Jazeera prove funding and independence can coexist?** For it: between 1996 and 2011 it demonstrably shifted regional norms and survived doing so. Against it: the 2011 resignation of director-general Wadah Khanfar amid WikiLeaks-published US diplomatic cables, the coverage tilt during the blockade, and the Arabic/English divergence all point the other way. The defensible position is narrow: independent enough to matter, not independent enough to serve as a check on its funder.

⚠️ **Contested: Globo and 1964.** That Globo lent editorial support to the coup is not seriously disputed. That it was decisive, or that its later dominance was *caused* by the favour, is. Historians differ on how much of Globo's rise was political payoff and how much was competent broadcasting and superior technology.

📄 **Primary source.** Compare one week of Al Jazeera English and Al Jazeera Arabic coverage of the same regional story. Note the framings, then note which audience each serves.

**Summary.** State media is not one thing. Monopoly, patronage, and party machinery each need a different countermeasure, and none of them is defeated by transparency alone.
