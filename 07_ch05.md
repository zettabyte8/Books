# 5. Ink: Print, Literacy, and the First Information War

**Objectives.** Explain why print was the first mass-influence technology; describe the Reformation pamphlet economy; identify the state's four standard responses.

**Key terms:** pamphlet · woodcut · index of prohibited books · official gazette · samizdat

---

Mass influence requires a medium that reaches strangers. Print was the first. Within fifty years of Gutenberg, presses across Europe were producing millions of sheets a year, and the price of a pamphlet fell below a day's wage.

The Reformation is the founding case, and it worked for reasons that will be familiar from Chapter 3. Luther's tracts were short, vernacular, and illustrated with woodcuts legible to the illiterate. They were also *reproducible* — a heretic could be burned, but a pamphlet could not be un-printed. Luther understood social proof: he wrote in the voice of "we," and the sheets carried woodcuts of the Pope as Antichrist. Estimates put his 1520 output at over 300,000 copies.

The state's response was immediate and set a template used ever since. Four moves, all still running:

1. **Licensing** — the English Star Chamber decree of 1586 required episcopal approval before printing.
2. **Monopoly** — the Stationers' Company was granted the exclusive right to print, making control commercial as well as political.
3. **The list** — the *Index Librorum Prohibitorum* (1559) named forbidden titles.
4. **The counter-voice** — official gazettes, from the French *Gazette* (1631) to the *London Gazette* (1665), supplied a sanctioned stream so the public need not seek illicit ones.

None of it worked cleanly. Print created a permanent smuggling economy and, with it, the reading public's habit of distrusting official print. Napoleon understood the residue: he suppressed Paris papers to four, then issued bulletins that inflated casualties in his favour — and the phrase "to lie like a bulletin" outlived him.

⚠️ **Contested: "print caused the Reformation."** Eisenstein's *The Printing Press as an Agent of Change* (1979) is the strong claim; later historians argue print amplified and accelerated a movement with deep economic and theological causes. Print was necessary and insufficient. Note that the same presses also carried the witch-hunt literature, which killed tens of thousands — a technology is not a direction.
