# 12. Compression: Indonesia, Nigeria, Turkey

**Objectives.** Define media compression; identify the three symptoms of a stacked register system; explain why the Western sequence does not generalise; assess one case in depth.

**Key terms:** compression · media oligarchy · platform-first election · licensing weapon · digital shutdown

---

Chapters 5 to 11 ran in sequence: print, then broadcast, then platforms, then regulation. That sequence is a Western artefact. In much of the world all four registers arrived within twenty years of each other, or arrived already stacked, and the sequence never happened.

Call it **compression**. Three symptoms follow, and they are the same three every time: an oligarch buys the broadcast register wholesale, a platform-first election arrives before the audience has any verification habit, and the licensing authority becomes the fastest available weapon because it was built for something else.

**Indonesia.** Suharto's fall in 1998 deregulated broadcasting almost overnight, and the licences went to conglomerates close to the outgoing order — a textbook patronage transfer, except that the internet arrived in the same decade and cut across it. Tapsell's *Media Power in Indonesia* (2017) maps the resulting oligarchy: a handful of families holding television, print, and political office simultaneously. The 2014 and 2019 elections were fought primarily on social media, with the 2019 campaign running through volunteer armies and celebrity endorsement; in 2024 the winning campaign deployed an AI-generated dancing avatar of the candidate in a pastel sweater, which worked because the audience had no prior expectation of what a campaign image should be. Regulation existed and was not the lever. The oligarch was.

**Nigeria.** The same compression with worse infrastructure. The 2019 and 2023 elections ran heavily through WhatsApp in a country where data is expensive and legacy media reach is thin; the National Assembly attempted a "fake news" bill in 2018 and the regulator issued a Code of Practice for platforms in 2022. The sharpest case is the 2021 suspension of Twitter after the platform deleted a presidential tweet — a ban lasting seven months, reversed after negotiation, executed in a fortnight because the licensing instrument already existed.

**Turkey.** Compression with the state ahead rather than behind. Broadcasting was deregulated in the 1990s into a commercial market, then re-regulated after 2016 into a compliant one: hundreds of press cards revoked, broadcasters taken over, RTÜK's powers extended, and Wikipedia blocked for three years from 2017 over a single article. Yesil's *Media in New Turkey* (2016) documents the pre-2016 trajectory. Here the sequence ran backwards — the platform arrived last and was treated as an intruder by an institutional register already built to close things.

**What compression explains.** The Western model assumes regulation lags and therefore needs catching up. Compressed systems have regulation first and reach second, so the regulator can close faster than the market can grow. It also explains why transparency law exports badly (→ 15): a disclosure obligation assumes a market with an audience that can read the disclosure. In a compressed system the audience is on a messaging app and the regulator is at the licence desk, and they never meet.

⚠️ **Contested: is "compression" a real concept or a convenient label?** It is not an established term in the literature — this chapter is proposing it. What is well documented is each component: oligarchic capture, platform-first elections, and licensing as a weapon all appear separately in the regional scholarship. Whether grouping them adds explanatory power beyond description is a live question, and the test is whether the grouping predicts anything. It should predict, for instance, that compressed systems will regulate platforms *through existing broadcast law* rather than through new platform law. Nigeria in 2021 is a test case in favour.

⚠️ **Contested: do these three belong together?** Turkey is a middle-income European-adjacent system with a functioning commercial press for most of its history; Indonesia and Nigeria are much poorer and much larger. The objection is that the comparison is geographic rather than structural. The defence is that all three share the timing variable, which is the variable the chapter claims matters. Test it by finding a compressed system where licensing is *not* the weapon.
