# 11. Airwaves and Ethnicity: Africa Beyond Rwanda

**Objectives.** Analyse three African broadcast cases; distinguish structural from episodic interference; assess the limits of the "external actor" explanation.

**Key terms:** editorial interference · public broadcaster capture · election annulment · shortwave · vernacular broadcasting

---

Rwanda (→ 8) is the African case everyone cites and the one most often used as a single data point. Three others are more instructive because they end differently.

**South Africa.** The SABC is a public broadcaster with a strong post-apartheid mandate and a documented record of political pressure. In 2016 the corporation's COO issued an instruction that protest coverage be restricted — no cameras at demonstrations, no commentary from "experts" — and journalists who objected were suspended and, in some cases, dismissed. They became the "SABC 8." The Western Cape High Court ruled in 2017 that the dismissals were unlawful and ordered reinstatement. The case matters because the interference was *written down*, which made it litigable. Editors' forums such as SANEF had something to take to court.

**Kenya.** Two elections, two mechanisms. Cambridge Analytica worked on Jubilee campaigns in 2013 and 2017, branding and segmentation in the first, more aggressive messaging in the second; Channel 4's 2018 *Dispatches* reporting exposed the firm's Kenyan footprint alongside its wider work. In August 2017 the Supreme Court annulled the presidential result outright — the first annulment in the region's history — citing failures in the transmission of results. The annulment was about the counting system, not the messaging, which is a useful corrective: the digital layer that mattered most was logistics, not persuasion.

**Ethiopia.** The hardest case. Under the EPRDF, the state broadcaster EBC held a near-monopoly and used it as such; shortwave and diaspora satellite filled the gap, and the 2008 press law was notoriously restrictive. The 2018 opening under Abiy Ahmed liberalised licensing and the 2021 Mass Media Proclamation replaced the old law — then the 2020–22 Tigray war produced blackouts and restricted access again. Ethiopia shows the whole arc in fifteen years: monopoly, opening, reversal.

**Vernacular radio** is the connective tissue and the reason the Rwandan comparison does not transfer cleanly. Most African broadcasting is multilingual and local, and local stations are cheap, trusted, and unmonitored. That is the opposite of RTLM's profile — a single national signal with a capital-city newsroom. The smaller the station, the less documented the interference and the more it matters to its listeners.

⚠️ **Contested: is interference structural or episodic?** The structural reading says public broadcasters are captured by design — appointments, budget, and licence renewal do the work, and each scandal is a symptom. The episodic reading says most output is unremarkable and interference clusters around elections and one or two ministries. The SABC record supports the first reading; the day-to-day output of most stations supports the second. Both are usually true at once, and the testable question is what changes when a court intervenes.

⚠️ **Contested: how much credit does Cambridge Analytica deserve in Kenya?** Almost certainly less than the coverage implies, for the reasons in Chapter 12 — Kalla and Broockman's null result applies to the same technique. The 2017 annulment turned on transmission failures, and the firm's own Kenya work was never independently evaluated.

📄 **Primary source.** The 2017 Western Cape High Court judgment in the SABC 8 matter. Read the finding on the COO's editorial instruction. Note that a written instruction is what made the case winnable.

**Summary.** Interference is easiest to fight where it is documented and hardest to see where the station is small. Kenya's annulment came from the counting system; South Africa's win came from a written instruction; Ethiopia's arc shows the window opening and closing.
