# 9. Patronage: The Gulf and Latin America

**Objectives.** Distinguish three patronage arrangements of state media; explain why carriage control substitutes for disclosure law; assess the Al Jazeera case; analyse the 2017 Qatar media crisis as a hinge event.

**Key terms:** state monopoly broadcaster · cadena · carriage control · clientelism · must-carry

---

Everything in Chapters 5 to 8 happened in Europe or the United States, where disclosure law is at least nominally present. Most of the world runs without it. The four architectures of Chapter 2 do not disappear — they invert. Interface control is heavy, institutional disclosure is thin, and the decisive lever is not transparency but **carriage**: who gets the slot, the frequency, the cable package.

This chapter covers three arrangements that trade on patronage. Chapter 10 adds a fourth that does not. Chapter 12 shows what happens when all four arrive at once.

**1. State monopoly broadcaster.** Venezuela's *cadenas* are the sharpest example: mandatory simultaneous broadcast of the president, pre-empting every other channel, which media monitors counted in the dozens of hours per week at their peak under Chávez (Cañizález). The mechanism needs no argument and no disclosure — it needs a transmitter and a decree. Soviet agitprop (→ 6) is the ancestor, and the *must-carry* rule is the plumbing.

**2. State-funded, editorially semi-independent.** Al Jazeera, launched in 1996 on the Emir of Qatar's money, broke the regional taboo on criticising Arab governments and carried the 2011 uprisings when state broadcasters would not. Sakr's *Satellite Realms* (2001) and Zayani's *Al Jazeera Phenomenon* (2005) both treat it as a genuine rupture in regional norms, not merely a rich man's mouthpiece; Kraidy's work on Arab hypermedia makes the same case from the audience side. It also demonstrated the arrangement's ceiling: it was instrumentalised during the 2017 blockade, its Arabic and English services diverge, and it remains structurally reluctant to bite the hand.

**3. Private monopoly in a clientelist deal.** Here the state owns nothing and owes something. Brazil's Globo built a near-national audience under military rule and, by the account of Brazilian media scholars from Sodré onward, lent it — Herz's *A História Secreta da Rede Globo* (1987) documents the debt structure, and Porto's *Media Power and Democratization in Brazil* (2012) traces what followed. Mexico's Televisa reached a documented 2012 arrangement with the Peña Nieto campaign covering favourable coverage, reported in the *New York Times* in 2014 and analysed at length in Lawson's *Building the Fourth Estate* (2002). The lever is the licence, the debt, and the advertising book — not the statute.

**The hinge case.** In May 2017, planted quotes attributed to the Emir appeared on Qatar's state news agency site. Within days Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Bahrain, and Egypt had severed relations and shut Al Jazeera bureaux; Ulrichsen's *Qatar and the Gulf Crisis* (2020) and Krieg's *Divided Gulf* (2019) treat the episode as a case study in media as casus belli. Nothing in Chapters 7 or 8 has an equivalent: a forged wire story producing a blockade in seventy-two hours.

**The lesson.** A reader trained only on Digital Services Act transparency (→ 15) will systematically over-read a WhatsApp election and under-read a licence renewal. Chapter 2's warning applies at full force: analyse one register and you under-report the pressure.

⚠️ **Contested: does Al Jazeera prove funding and independence can coexist?** For it: between 1996 and 2011 it demonstrably shifted regional norms and survived doing so. Against it: the 2011 resignation of director-general Wadah Khanfar amid WikiLeaks-published US diplomatic cables, the coverage tilt during the blockade, and the Arabic/English divergence all point the other way. The defensible position is narrow: independent enough to matter, not independent enough to serve as a check on its funder.

⚠️ **Contested: Globo and 1964.** That Globo lent editorial support to the coup is not seriously disputed. That it was decisive, or that its later dominance was *caused* by the favour, is. Porto and others differ on how much of Globo's rise was political payoff and how much was competent broadcasting and superior technology.
