# 4. Why Campaigns Fail: The Ledger of Unintended Outcomes

**Objectives.** Read the outcome ledger critically; define blowback and second-order effect; explain why short-term success and long-term failure coexist; work an Overton case in each direction.

**Key terms:** second-order effect · blowback · credibility gap · overton window · boomerang effect

---

Campaigns are judged by their designers on immediate compliance. History judges them on what came after. The gap between those two ledgers is the most reliable pattern in this book, and it is nearly always the same shape: **the tactic works, and the working is what costs.**

| Campaign | Method | Wanted | Got |
|---|---|---|---|
| Creel Committee, 1917–19 | Saturation, Four Minute Men, atrocity posters | War support | Bonds sold; German-American vigilantism; postwar cynicism about all official messaging |
| Torches of Freedom, 1929 | Staged debutante march, suffrage framing | Female smokers | Market opened; a century of lung disease |
| Lysenkoism, 1930s–60s | Purges, censorship of genetics | Ideological biology | Soviet genetics destroyed; harvests lost |
| Nudge units, 2010s– | Defaults, norm comparisons | Compliance | 5–15% on narrow tasks; little against structural problems |
| Engagement optimisation, 2010s– | Predictive ranking | Retention and ad revenue | Revenue; outrage, distrust, teen mental-health decline |

Three mechanisms generate these.

**Blowback.** The method discredits itself by being seen. Creel's committee was so effective that "propaganda" became a dirty word in the 1920s and Bernays renamed his trade "public relations." The 1967 exposure of CIA funding for the Congress for Cultural Freedom damaged the very journals the money had built.

**Second-order effects.** A campaign optimised on one metric imports that metric's blind spots. Rank on engagement and you rank outrage, because outrage is what people click. Nobody chose polarisation; it was a by-product of a proxy.

**Credibility gap.** Once a population learns it is being managed, honest campaigns inherit the suspicion. This is the most expensive legacy, and it compounds across generations.

### The window in practice

Chapter 3 said a shifted window delivers a programme while a won argument delivers a decision. Two cases show it, in opposite directions.

**Forward: same-sex marriage in the United States.** In 1996 a defence-of-marriage act passed with broad support and roughly a quarter of the public in favour. The strategy that moved the window was explicit and sequenced: litigation in friendly states first, ballot-measure losses absorbed rather than avoided, and a deliberate reframing from "civil unions" to "why marriage" by Freedom to Marry from 2003. Massachusetts legalised in 2004; the national window closed behind it; the Supreme Court followed in 2015. Nobody won a national argument first. They changed what counted as arguable, then won.

**Backward: carbon pricing in the Republican Party.** A policy with conservative intellectual parentage — the 1997 Byrd–Hagel resolution, McCain's 2008 platform — became apostasy within four years, largely through the triangulation move: the centre was redefined so cap-and-trade sat outside it. The policy did not get refuted. It got made tribal, and then it got boring.

**The lesson, and the limit.** Both cases show sequencing doing work that persuasion could not. Neither shows the window alone being sufficient — marriage equality still needed a court, and carbon pricing still has no statute. A shifted window is an opening, not an outcome.

⚠️ **Contested: is blowback universal?** No. The British wartime rationing campaigns (→ 8) produced almost none, because the sacrifice was visible, shared, and explained. Blowback tracks *covertness and asymmetry*, not persuasion as such. A campaign that says what it is doing and why is largely immune.

**Check.** (1) Distinguish a second-order effect from a failure. (2) Why did rationing escape blowback? (3) Which of the three window moves was used in the carbon case? (4) Why is a shifted window not an outcome?

🔧 **Activity.** Take one school policy — uniform, phone ban, attendance incentive. Predict its second-order effects on paper. Then interview three students and compare. Predictions will be too optimistic. Then identify one window move the school could make and cost it.

**Summary.** Success and harm are not opposites; harm is frequently success's residue. Windows move by sequencing, and they move in both directions.
